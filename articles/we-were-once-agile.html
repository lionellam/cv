We Were Once Agile

And Then We Were Not

There is a strange symmetry to human history: we were agile, then we were not, and now, as it is starting to appear, we are becoming agile again. The pendulum is appearing to swing the other way.

For most of our species' existence, agility was not a virtue we aspired to. It was simply the condition of life — a way of being. Hunter-gatherer bands were small, mobile, and loosely structured. They dispersed and regrouped with the seasons, the herds, the rains. Decisions were made close to the ground, by the people who would live with the consequences. There was no distance between knowledge and action, because there was no hierarchy long enough to create one.

Then came the city — and with it, a different kind of human project. Agglomeration solved real problems: surplus needed storing, storage needed defending, defense needed coordinating. But coordination at scale demanded structure, and structure has a way of calcifying into permanence — one that is self-perpetuating. Walls, granaries, bureaucracies, org charts. Each solved a coordination problem by inserting a layer between the person and the thing being coordinated — a scribe between the harvest and the granary, a manager between the worker and the work. Over thousands of years we built ever-larger systems of coordination for organizing human effort, and the price of their power was almost always the same: distance, manufactured one abstraction at a time. Distance between the decision and the ground it would land on. Distance between the worker and the meaning of the work. Each layer was added to make the system legible to itself — so that a distant authority could "see" a harvest, a workforce, a city, without having to stand in it — and each layer of legibility cost a layer of contact with the thing itself. We traded the responsiveness of the campfire for the might of the agglomeration, and we paid for it in abstraction.

It's tempting to think of the last two centuries — the factory, the corporation, the nation-state — as the natural endpoint of that trajectory. But something has started to bend backward. Organizations are flattening. Work is distributing. Cities are no longer the only place where opportunity concentrates. Remote teams span time zones the way foraging bands once spanned river valleys, coordinating not through proximity but through shared intent. We call this "agile" now, and we treat it as an invention — conceived in a 2001 manifesto — but it might, perhaps, be more appropriate to call it a remembering. We are re-discovering, with great effort (and expensive consultants), a way of working that our ancestors never had to learn, because they had never lost it.

This appears to be the inflection point we are standing on: a slow unwinding of the centralizing logic that built the modern world in the first place. And it is arriving at a moment shadowed by VUCA (Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity and Ambiguity), and by a mounting sense that the growth model of the last two centuries cannot be sustained indefinitely. The dispersal happening now is not nostalgic. It is being forced, in part, by the recognition that concentration — of people, of resources, of consumption — has costs we deferred but never erased.

Technology is the great accelerant and the great confounder in this story. It has given us decades of additional life, instant communication across oceans, and a scale of possibility our ancestors would have called divine. We are living longer, healthier, more informationally saturated lives than at any point in human history. And yet the same technology that dissolved the old distances has built a new one: the distance between experience and its representation.

There is a subtler abstraction happening underneath this one, and it may be the most consequential of all: the conflation and reduction of subjectivity at scale into something that presents itself as objective measurement. A single person's satisfaction, pain, or effort is a felt, particular, unrepeatable thing. But aggregate a million of them — through a survey, a star rating, a sentiment score, a productivity metric — and something strange happens. The particularity is averaged away, and what remains gets treated as fact: a number on a dashboard, clean and comparable and seemingly free of the mess it was extracted from. This is not measurement in the way a thermometer measures temperature. It is interpolation: individual, incommensurable experiences smoothed into a single line on a chart (or spreadsheet) and then mistaken for the ground truth those experiences were merely evidence of.

There is a paradox buried even deeper than the metric, and it is one we rarely notice because we are standing inside it: the tool we use to name all of this — language itself — is already an abstraction. A word is never the "thing". Language reduces the continuous, sensory, unrepeatable texture of experience into discrete, reusable, transmissible symbols — that is precisely what makes it useful, but also precisely what makes it lossy. We then take that already-reductive tool and objectify it further: into forms, into fields in a database, into prompts and outputs on a screen. Each additional layer treats language as if it were the experience rather than a compression of it and forgets that the compression happened at all. The paradox of our existence may be this: the very medium we use to reach for meaning, connection, and understanding is abstract (and subjective) by construction, so every system we build on top of language inherits a reduction it can never fully undo.

Can the full complexity of a human life — its texture, its risk, its embodied uncertainty — be adequately captured by what we read off a pane of glass? A message thread is not a conversation. A dashboard is not a business, and an employee engagement score is not an employee. A video call is not a room. We have made remarkable progress in transmitting information about life, work, and relationship, but transmission is not the same as presence, and a metric derived from many subjectivities is not the same as understanding any one of them. The hunter-gatherer's agility was total — mind, body, and consequence moved together, with nothing interpolated between the two. Our digital agility is partial: it moves information, and increasingly moves inference about how people feel, at incredible speed, while leaving the body, and much of what the body knows, behind.

The job market is a particularly sharp place to watch this whole chain play out. A role is a bundle of tacit judgment, relationships, and situational skill that no sentence fully holds. It gets written down anyway, as a job description, fitting nicely enough to be legible on a computer screen or a piece of paper — a first compression, already lossy in the way all language is. But in recent years, that description is no longer written primarily to be read by a human hiring manager; it is written to be found, formatted to satisfy a search algorithm's sense of what the role is. Keywords are chosen for discoverability rather than accuracy. Titles are inflated or narrowed to fit a taxonomy the platform recognizes. This is epistemic distortion in a very literal sense: the description of the job starts to diverge from the job, shaped less by the work itself than by what a matching system can parse. Job matching platforms then complete the loop — they don't just host these descriptions, they industrialize the matching of them, turning what was once a human judgment (does this person seem right for this team?) into a scored, commoditized process performed at the scale of millions of postings and profiles simultaneously. Candidates learn to write resumes the same way employers learn to write postings: optimized for the algorithm's legibility first. Two already-reductive compressions of reality are matched against each other by a third abstraction, and everyone downstream inherits the distortion.

This is also where technology's double nature — accelerant at the core, constraint at the boundary — becomes visible. Inside an organization, a platform like this genuinely expands reach: a small team can now surface itself to a labor market that would once have been invisible to it. But at the interface, where the organization meets the platform, the relationship inverts. To be found at all, the organization must conform to the platform's categories, its keyword logic, its cadence of posting and refreshing listings. What began as a tool the organization uses becomes a protocol the organization is used by — an obligate technology, in the sense that opting out is no longer really an option if you want to compete. Teams and organizations then do what any system under selective pressure does: they adapt, locally and often very well, to the demands of the platform that mediates their access to talent, customers, or capital. The better they get at satisfying the platform's local logic, the more their internal language, incentives, and even their sense of what the role or the product is gets pulled toward what scores well rather than what is true. The advantage becomes the Achilles' heel. A team can be locally coherent — every job posting optimized, every metric trending the right way, every process legible to the system it's embedded in — while drifting steadily out of alignment with the larger, slower reality it was supposed to serve. Success within the paradigm becomes disembodied from the purpose it was meant to serve.

Perhaps that is the real work of this inflection point — not simply becoming agile again in the organizational sense, dispersing our teams and flattening our hierarchies, but stripping back some of the abstraction we built up along the way: fewer layers between the decision and the ground, fewer metrics standing in for the people they were sampled from, and systems built to human scale rather than to the scale abstraction makes possible. We have proven we can move fast, and we have proven we can measure almost anything. What remains to be seen is whether we can move, and measure, without losing hold of the particular, unrepeatable life each word and number was quietly compressed from.
